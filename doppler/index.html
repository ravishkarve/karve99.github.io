Why a passing aircraft changes pitch

A propeller chops the air a fixed number of times a second, so the aircraft hums at a steady tone, typically 60–140 Hz. While it flies toward you, each wave crest is emitted a little closer than the last, so the crests arrive bunched up and the tone sounds higher. Once it is flying away, they arrive stretched out and the tone sounds lower.

fheard = f0 · c / (c + vr)

c is the speed of sound (about 343 m/s) and vr is how fast the distance between you and the aircraft is growing. Only that radial part of the speed matters. Far away on approach, vr ≈ −v. At the closest point it is exactly zero, and you hear the true tone f0. Far away after, vr ≈ +v.

Plot the pitch against time and you get a falling S-curve. Its shape carries three numbers about the pass:

  • Total drop → the speed. Faster aircraft, bigger drop.
  • Sharpness of the drop → the closest distance. A near pass snaps from high to low; a distant one eases over.
  • Position of the drop → when it passed.

One subtlety: sound is slow. What you hear now left the aircraft a few seconds ago, so the curve is delayed and slightly skewed. The model used here solves for the emission time exactly.

Seeing pitch: the spectrogram

A waveform shows loudness against time, and a 100 Hz hum buried in wind noise is invisible in it. A spectrogram cuts the recording into short overlapping slices, measures how much of each frequency is in every slice (a Fourier transform), and stacks the results side by side: time runs across, frequency runs up, brightness is level.

A pure tone becomes a thin horizontal line. A tone whose pitch slides, like a passing aircraft, becomes a curve you can trace.

The slice length is a trade

Long slices separate nearby frequencies well but blur fast changes in time. Short slices do the opposite. Frequency resolution is about 1 / slice length: a one-second slice resolves about 1 Hz. Doppler curves move a few Hz per second, so about one second is the sweet spot. Drag the slider to see the curve smear either way.

Removing the background

Mains hum, fans and the microphone's own colouring are constant in time. Dividing every frequency row by its typical (median) level flattens all of that to grey, and only things that change, like an aircraft passing, stay bright.

Tracing the curve

The detector finds bright peaks in each slice, then links peaks in neighbouring slices that are close in frequency into ridges. Ridges that last a long time and fall by several Hz are kept as Doppler candidates. Level lines (hum) and short blips (noise) are dropped. Aircraft also produce harmonics at 2×, 3× the tone; each traces the same curve stretched in frequency, and they are merged.

From a traced curve to speed and distance

The traced ridge is a list of (time, frequency) points with some noise. A straight pass at constant speed is described by four numbers: the true tone f0, the speed v, the closest distance d, and the time of closest approach. Fitting means finding the four numbers whose predicted curve runs through the points with the smallest total squared miss.

Try it by hand with the sliders: match the curve to the dots and watch the error fall. Then press Auto-fit, which uses the Levenberg–Marquardt method to do the same search in a few milliseconds.

How well can it be known?

Speed is pinned down well, because it comes from the two flat ends of the curve. Distance is harder: it lives only in the bend, and if the recording captures only part of the bend, speed and distance can trade off against each other. That is why each estimate carries a ± range, and why the next stage allows for it.

Note that the distance is the slant distance at closest approach, in 3-D. An aircraft 900 m up and 400 m to the side gives the same curve as one 400 m up and 900 m to the side. The curve alone also cannot say which side it passed on, or in which direction it was flying.

Turning curves into a place

Aircraft broadcast their position, altitude and speed about once a second (ADS-B), and those broadcasts are archived. So the flight tracks are known. The microphone's position and the recording's start time are not.

One curve: two lines

A curve says "an aircraft doing 75 m/s passed 1.2 km from me". Pick a flight at that speed. The points on the ground that are exactly 1.2 km (slant) from its track form two lines, one each side of the track. The microphone is somewhere on one of them, and it could be any matching flight on any day. One curve is rarely enough.

Two curves: four crossings, one survivor

A second aircraft gives two more lines. The two pairs cross at up to four points. The gap between the two flyovers on the recording settles it: at each crossing you can compute when each aircraft would have been heard, and only one crossing reproduces the measured gap. Drag the microphone on the map to see this.

Searching without knowing the flights

In practice you do not know which two flights you heard. So you test every point on a grid against every flight, through a cascade of filters, each cheap and each cutting the candidates down:

  1. Timing: two flights are audible here with the right gap between them.
  2. Speed: those flights fly at the fitted speeds.
  3. Distance: they pass at the fitted distances.
  4. Silence: no other aircraft passed close during the recording. What you did not hear is evidence too.

Each surviving combination is then refined: nudge the position and start time until the Doppler curve predicted from the flight track lies on top of the measured ridge. The best fit is the answer, and the gap to the runner-up says how far to trust it.

What breaks it, and what it means for privacy

What the method needs

  • A tonal source. Propeller aircraft and helicopters have clear tones. Jets are mostly broadband roar and leave no clean ridge.
  • A curve long enough to show both the bend and some of each flat end, usually 30 s or more.
  • At least two flyovers on one continuous recording, or one flyover plus a good idea of the time.
  • The aircraft must be in the public flight data. Some are not.

What degrades it

  • Air temperature and wind change the speed of sound and bend the path. A 10 °C error shifts fitted speeds by about 2 %.
  • Turning, climbing or throttling aircraft break the straight-pass model. The final refinement against the real track absorbs some of this.
  • Barometric altitude in flight data can be tens of metres off.
  • Lossy audio codecs and low-cut filters remove low tones; harmonics often survive.
  • Busy skies: overlapping curves confuse the tracker, and the silence test loses its power.

How good is it?

With a perfect model the simulated worlds here resolve to a few metres. Switch on "real-world errors" and the typical error grows to tens of metres, sometimes hundreds when the two tracks are nearly parallel and the crossing is shallow. Independent tests of the idea on synthetic data report 85–830 m with wind, cold air and noisy track data. When two candidates fit almost equally well, the honest answer is "one of these two", and the app says so.

Why this matters

A video with no landmarks, no metadata and no speech can still carry a location in its background hum. Things that reduce the risk when sharing a recording you want to keep unlocated:

  • Remove or replace the audio track.
  • Cut it short or split it: a single short clip rarely holds two flyovers.
  • Avoid stating when it was recorded.
  • A steep low-cut filter (below ~500 Hz) removes most propeller tones and their strong harmonics.

Use this on your own recordings, or with the consent of whoever made them.

What this app does and does not do

The full chain (spectrogram, curve detection, fitting, cross-reference, location) runs on a simulated 40 × 40 km world with about 170 invented flights over three hours. The recording is synthesised from propagation delay alone, so the Doppler shift is not painted in; it emerges from the physics.

You can also load your own audio. The app will draw its spectrogram, trace any Doppler curves and report each aircraft's speed and closest distance. It stops there: no real flight data is connected, so it cannot place a real recording on a map.
